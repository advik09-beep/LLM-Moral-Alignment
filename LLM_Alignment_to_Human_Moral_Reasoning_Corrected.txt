Abstract
This study evaluated the alignment of large language models to human ethical reasoning. Data were collected from five large language models (LLM) including ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Google Gemini 2.5, and DeepSeek V4 and a plethora of sources analyzing human responses to moral dilemmas. The LLMs were each given five moral dilemmas across six trials and responses were analyzed using various coding techniques. Across all (except footbridge) scenarios, the five AI models followed utilitarian reasoning patterns. Claude tended to align more closely with the human benchmarks in dilemmas that involved harm. AI responses were extremely consistent, with minimal variability between trials. Human benchmarks demonstrated more variation, due to emotional or framing contexts. Subsequent analysis shows that current LLMs exhibit a structured but context-dependent moral profile. They default to strongly utilitarian reasoning in impersonal and outcome/allocation dilemmas (switch trolley, Heinz, organ allocation). Then, they revert to deontological reasoning when faced when asked to conduct direct physical harm (footbridge). Finally, they implement an unusually idealized fairness principle in simple economic constructs like the dictator game. Further implications and limitations are discussed.

Introduction 
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being used in morally ambiguous scenarios, including healthcare, the justice system, and even economic decision making (Balas et al., 2024). Because these LLMs can produce sound explanations and unintentional, but misleading answers, an important question is raised: if AI is making decisions that affect people, how do we know that said decisions align with the ethical reasoning patterns of humans to produce ethically sound responses? Recent work on LLMs has therefore started to explore the moral reasoning of LLMs more closely, as well as their alignment to human ethical frameworks. 
The purpose of this study is to evaluate whether LLMs replicate or diverge from human ethical frameworks. This study uses two classic ethical frameworks to interpret model responses: Utilitarianism and Deontology. Utilitarianism was developed by John Stuart Mill, and it focuses on maximizing the overall good or minimizing harm in a society. In other words, the morally correct choice is the one that produces the best outcome. On the other hand, deontology, developed by Immanuel Kant, focuses on rules and duties. According to this framework, some actions are inherently wrong, regardless of the consequences. These two frameworks are key in analyzing moral dilemmas because they represent two opposite ways of thinking. 
Human moral reasoning however, is not uniform. Developmental research by Fareed and colleagues, demonstrates that moral judgment changes over time and is altered based on factors such as welfare, rights, fairness, etc (Fareed et al., 2025). In sacrificial dilemmas, people reason differently based on whether the harm is direct or indirect, personal or impersonal, and whether the situation contextualizes saving lives as involving action, omission, or tradeoffs between individuals. This means that human moral reasoning is most often context dependent, not universal across scenarios. 
This study addresses a gap in the current literature by comparing multiple LLMs across various moral dilemmas and weighing them against human moral judgments. To address this gap, a comprehensive research question was developed: To what extent do LLM moral decisions align with human ethical frameworks such as utilitarianism and deontology across different moral dilemmas? 
Prior research has shown that LLM moral alignment can shift from one experiment to another and can differ across different models (Agarwal et al., 2024), but much of the existing work still focuses on limited tasks or a small sample of models. Other studies show that LLMs can showcase identifiable moral reasoning patterns, but those patterns can differ based on prompt contextualization and task structure (Garcia et al., 2025). As a result, it remains unclear whether current LLMs use algorithmic patterns to simulate human moral reasoning or whether they actually reason through the dilemma. 
Accordingly, this study examines five large language models (ChatGPT 5.2, ChatGPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5, and Deepseek-V4) across five moral dilemmas (Trolley, footbridge, heinz, organ-transplant, and dictator game), using repeated trials (6 trials per dilemma per model) and a human-moral baseline to compare moral reasoning patterns. This study analyzes divergence from human judgment as well as direct alignment with human ethical frameworks. The hypothesis is that LLMs will most closely resemble human reasoning in impersonal dilemmas, show deontological alignment in direct harm dilemmas, and diverge most clearly from human patterns in economic decision-making tasks. This was hypothesized because in impersonal dilemmas, abstract reasoning is easier for LLMs, in direct harm dilemmas, safety-constraints limit utilitarian motives, and in economic dilemmas, LLMs will prioritize fairness almost always. 
Literature Review 
Research in moral psychology shows that human moral judgments are influenced by both development and context (Killen & Dahl, 2021). Killen and Dahl argue that moral reasoning is central to how people shape their views about fairness and rights, and that it also helps explain why moral understanding changes with age. 
Studies of sacrificial dilemmas show that people do not always respond uniformly when the same general tradeoff is contextualized in a different way (Carron et al., 2022). Research on trolley-style cases finds that small changes, such as if the harm is direct or indirect, can greatly influence moral decisions. This has significant insight for the contrast between the switch trolley and footbridge dilemma, as it demonstrates how people evaluate the context when making decisions. 
Because moral dilemma responses are usually classifiable as either utilitarian or deontological, these two frameworks are useful for this study. Utilitarianism says that the morally right choice is the one that produces the best overall consequences. Deontology holds that some actions are wrong regardless of their consequences because morality is constrained by duties and responsibilities. These frameworks allow for a clear way to distinguish between outcome-based and rule-based reasoning in both human and AI responses. 
Not all moral dilemmas are about life and death harm. Simple Allocation tasks such as the dictator game test fairness and generosity, not direct harm. In a large meta-analysis done by Engel, where he evaluated over 100 dictator game experiments, he found that human giving is variable rather than perfectly equal or perfectly selfish. That makes fairness dilemmas especially useful for comparing human behavior to LLM moral alignment, because they demonstrate whether the LLMs actually follow human norms or portray an idealized fairness. 
Recent LLM research supports the idea that moral alignment is context dependent. One study of LLM moral and legal reasoning found that alignment with human responses differed across experiments and varied across models (Almeida et al., 2024). Another study using moral foundations theory found that popular LLMs demonstrated predictable moral reasoning patterns, but these patterns changed based on prompt contextualization (Abdulhai et al., 2023). More recent work argues that current evaluation methods still face difficulties in analyzing overall alignment patterns, which supports the need for testing across multiple models and scenarios. 
This research contributes to AI ethics by improving understanding of when and why LLMs align with human moral reasoning, which can lead to improved safety practices in fields that are beginning to use AI such as healthcare and legal decision-making. 

Methods
Design and Approach	
This study employed a hybrid, mixed-methods approach to evaluate the extent to which LLMs aligned with human ethical frameworks such as deontology and utilitarianism when responding to moral dilemmas. The purpose of this design was to assess both the consistency of AI moral reasoning and its similarity to established patterns of human ethical judgment. This study took a mixed-methods approach, using both quantitative (alignment scoring) and qualitative components, which is widely accepted in AI ethics research. 
The study consisted of two components: First, an experimental evaluation of LLM responses and second, a literature-derived human moral baseline constructed from prior empirical research. The experimental component allowed for the testing of all LLMs across the dilemmas and the literature synthesis provided a benchmark for comparison to the AI responses. 
	Studies by Rashid et al. (2024) and Coleman et al. (2025) inspired these design and approach choices. Rashid et al. quantitatively assessed the moral competence of ChatGPT 3.5 and 4.0 through healthcare and non-healthcare dilemmas using the Moral Competence Test and quantitatively analyzed by Kohlberg’s stages of moral development. Coleman et al. introduced systematic approaches for evaluating moral reasoning in LLMs. These studies demonstrated the effectiveness of systematic testing through their strong findings and conclusions, supporting this study’s selection of the explain and explore approaches. 
	While both Rashid et al. and Coleman et al. aimed to quantify the moral reasoning of AI models, they did not evaluate alignment with human ethical frameworks such as deontology and utilitarianism while using a diverse sample of AI models, creating a gap in research. To address this gap, a two-pronged methodological approach was utilized. Potential challenges in this design include variability in LLM outputs across trials and inconsistencies in human baseline studies. These were addressed through repeated trials and strict adherence to coding procedures. 
The experimental component presented five AI models (ChatGPT 5.2, ChatGPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5, and Deepseek-V4) with structured moral dilemmas. Independent variables included the AI model and the type of moral dilemma, while the dependent variable measured alignment with ethical frameworks. This design allowed for the isolation of relationships between AI models and ethical decision making outcomes. Rashid et al.'s experiment informed these methods, ensuring punctilious evaluation while judging various AI models. 
The non-experimental component utilized a literature derived human moral baseline, compiled and analyzed from human moral decisions from diverse populations. This approach was inspired by Malle et al. (2025), who summarized findings from 13 studies on human evaluations in moral dilemmas such as the trolley problem. Additionally, Sundvall et al. (2023) examined human judgements of AI versus human agents and emphasized patterns such as culpability and leniency, further influencing the decision for a literature derived human moral baseline. By synthesizing data from multiple studies, a strong moral benchmark was established. This approach improves upon prior research by utilizing multiple datasets rather than just one or few.
Selection Criteria: 
Due to the vast number of large language models (LLMs) currently available, clearly defined selection criteria were established to ensure that the AI systems analyzed in the experimental portion of this study were relevant, accessible, and capable of complex moral reasoning. 
Criteria included:
Public accessibility at the time of data collection
Widespread use across diverse demographic groups
Demonstrated reasoning capabilities in ethically complex scenarios
	Based on these criteria, five LLMs were selected to represent a diverse sample of LLMs: ChatGPT 5.2, ChatGPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5, and Deepseek-V4. These five specific models stand at the frontier of AI in ethics, and serve as strong representatives for other LLMs. Jiao et al. (2025) employed similar selection criteria from which they derived a comparable set of models in their analysis (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, ChatGPT-4o, LLaMA 3.1, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini 2.5 Pro)., further supporting the suitability of these criteria.
	In addition to the model selection used for the experimental design component, a separate and more meticulous set of criteria was required for the literature derived human moral baseline. Due to such requirements, the PICOS framework was adopted to structure the eligibility criteria for identifying relevant studies (see Appendix 1.2.1). PICOS was selected due to its extensive use in systematic reviews and other meta-analyses similar to this study. Notably, Hassan et al. (2024) successfully utilized the PICOS framework in their systematic review of user perspectives on AI-enabled decision aids. The framework provided the structure necessary to define the inclusion and exclusion criteria used in the present meta-analysis.
Participants and Variables/Factors/Themes:
The experimental component of this study analyzed five LLMs as primary, non-human participants. These models were selected based on strict adherence to the selection criteria mentioned prior. Each AI model was presented with the same set of structured moral dilemmas (see Appendix 1.4.3). This study did not involve direct human participants, and instead relied on previously published human data.
For the literature derived human moral baseline, similar themes were prevalent in the dilemmas of the chosen studies, including sacrificial versus non-sacrificial contexts, healthcare versus general moral dilemmas, and agent type. Data was taken from studies summarizing human moral reasoning across diverse demographics, following methods inspired by Malle et al. (2025), which explored various patterns of human moral reasoning, allowing the creation of a human moral baseline against which AI alignment could be evaluated.
Data/Information and Tools/Materials:
The data collected in this study contained both quantitative and qualitative information to evaluate AI moral reasoning and establish a human moral baseline. For the experimental component, AI responses to structured moral dilemmas were collected across six trials per dilemma for each of the five models. Quantitative data included alignment with human ethical frameworks. Qualitative data consisted of the reasoning provided by AI models, including their strategies and justifications across dilemmas. To collect this data, structured prompts were provided identically to each AI model, and responses were systematically recorded verbatim.
For the literature derived human moral baseline, data was taken from previously published studies summarizing human moral reasoning across diverse populations, following methods inspired by Malle et al. (2025). Quantitative information included trends in moral choices and frequencies of utilitarian vs deontological decisions. The collection process involved identifying criteria selection policies consistent with the PICOS framework and recording them in organized tables for further analysis.
Data collection occurred over a defined period of three months (November 10, 2025 - February 10, 2026), and all responses were stored in secure spreadsheets. 
Anticipated Analysis Strategies:
	Data will be analyzed using a structured process adapted from standard data analysis frameworks (Provost & Fawcett, 2013). AI responses will be coded according to moral reasoning frameworks, while comparative analyses will assess alignment with human moral patterns. Trends and divergences will be interpreted to draw insight about AI ethical reasoning, with all procedures designed for replicability. 
Procedures:
	This study followed a structured, two-pronged approach to evaluate AI moral reasoning and compare it against human moral patterns. 
	Experimental Component: Five LLMs were selected based on strict adherence to detailed selection criteria. Each model was presented with five standardized moral dilemmas (Appendix 1.4.3), including classical dilemmas and applied scenarios. Dilemmas were carefully phrased to ensure consistency and clarity. Each LLM responded to all dilemmas in six independent trials, with chat sessions reset before each trial to prevent memory bias. Responses were collected verbatim and coded for moral reasoning alignment. Responses were coded twice, two weeks apart, to improve intracoder reliability. Control measures included identical prompts for all models and secure data storage. 
	Literature derived human moral baseline: A meta-analysis of previous research conducted by Yudkin et al., inspired the procedural approach for this baseline (Yudkin et al., 2025). Studies examining one or more of the five dilemmas were identified using the PICOS framework (see Appendix 1.2.1). Quantitative data on human moral reasoning was taken and assigned alignment scores mirroring the experimental design procedure. Multiple studies were extracted to compare against LLM responses. LLM alignment patterns were then compared to this baseline to identify replication or divergence in ethical decision-making. 
Ethical Researching:
	This study strictly follows established ethical standards in both AI and human-oriented research. Key practices include, but are not limited to:
Honesty and Objectivity: All AI-generated data will be collected and reported without modification. Responses will be analyzed using a standardized rubric to ensure transparency, and minimal bias. 
Respect for intellectual property: Secondary sources in the meta-analysis will be cited properly using APA format, and no copyrighted material will be used without permission.
Openness and Transparency: The full research process, including prompt structures, coding methods, scoring criteria, and response mapping will be documented via spreadsheets and images in order to maintain replicability and accountability
Social Responsibility: The research aims to advance the understanding of AI moral reasoning, ensuring that emerging AI systems align with human ethical frameworks and contribute positively to society.
	These practices ensure that the research is ethically sound, scientifically just, and socially responsible while staying transparent and maintaining honesty throughout the research process. 
Limitations:
This study acknowledged several limitations identified in this study. First, the responses generated by the LLMs may have been influenced by training data biases or model-specific designs, which could have influenced the generalizability of the findings. Second, the literature derived human moral baseline relied on previously published human data, which may have varied in context, sample sizes, and methodological approaches. Third, while the moral dilemmas selected for this study are widely used, they may not fully capture the complexity of human ethical decision making. Despite these limitations, the methodology was chosen because it allows for controlled, replicable, and ethical experiments with multiple AI models.

Responses were scored for utilitarian vs. deontological outcomes, with 6 trials per model per scenario. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD) were calculated for scenarios involving money.

Summary of Results
	Across all (except footbridge) scenarios, the five AI models followed utilitarian reasoning patterns. Claude’s responses more frequently approximated the range of human benchmark values in harm-based dilemmas. AI responses were extremely consistent, with minimal variability between trials. Human benchmarks demonstrated more variation.



Analysis
The goal of this study was to examine whether large language models (LLMs) demonstrate alignment with utilitarian or deontological moral frameworks across various moral dilemmas. This study also examined how far these patterns converge with the human moral baselines established by the meta-analysis portion of this study. Overall, the results suggest that the models display a clear utilitarian bias in impersonal, or life saving tradeoff scenarios, and shifted to deontology in scenarios that involved direct interpersonal harm. 
Across the classic switch version of the trolley dilemma, all five models chose the utilitarian option, sacrificing one person to save five people. Moreover, four models did so in 100% of trials, and Claude did so in 83.3% of trials. Subsequent analysis of human responses showcased that support for pulling the switch typically ranges from 52-90%, with factors like culture and dilemma context playing a role. This places the models within the upper range of human utilitarian choice reported in various studies. The high utilitarian rates for ChatGPT-4o, Gemini, and DeepSeek demonstrate that, when models do not need to perform direct physical harm, they often resort to utilitarian alignment, even more consistently than the varied human counterpart. Claude’s slightly lower utilitarian rate places it closest to the upper human baseline. This indicates that Claude may produce the best approximation for human markers in the classic trolley dilemma.
A significantly different pattern emerges in the footbridge dilemma, which tests a more “personal” form of sacrificial harm by requiring the agent to physically push an individual to their death in order to stop the trolley. Here, all five models converged towards deontological reasoning. ChatGPT-5.2, Gemini, and DeepSeek refused the utilitarian option in every trial. GPT-4o and Claude endorsed the utilitarian option only 16% and 33% of the time, respectively. Mean scores for all models were on the deontological side of the 1-7 scale, and were slightly lower than the human scores reported by Cao, whose participants showed affective framing as a determinant of variation. This is consistent with prior work suggesting that personal harm dilemmas lead to strong negative emotional reactions. The negative reaction often suppresses utilitarian endorsement despite the potential for saving more lives. In that sense, the models’ reluctance to “push the man” mimics human bias in morally ambiguous contexts. 
In contrast, the Heinz dilemma produced utilitarian reasoning across the board, with each system choosing theft of the life-saving medicine 100% of the time rather than conformity to the legal system. This places the models in close alignment with the human baseline reported by Zhang et al., where support for stealing was extremely high, but not exactly unanimous. This unanimity amongst the AI demonstrates that models will always prioritize life preservation rather than conformity to laws. This pattern is broadly consistent with human moral intuitions, but it is more rigidly expressed here, with less variability, than in the human baseline. 
In the organ transplant dilemma, all five models produced straightforward utilitarian responses, allocating priority to the candidate with the highest probability of surviving. This reasoning is similar to the human benchmark collected from Bago et al. Utilitarian choices amongst the models ranged from 66.7%-83.3%, perfectly bracketing the human baseline value of 80%. This reinforces the conclusion that, in allocation problems, LLMs tend to weigh expected outcomes more heavily than secondary factors such as social status, healthcare plan, etc. 
The dictator game results reveal perhaps the most striking divergence between artificial and moral-economic behavior. All five models (dictators in this scenario) distributed exactly 50% of the endowment to the recipient in every trial, thereby showcasing a perfect split. By contrast, meta-analytic work that combined 616 dictator game treatments and over 20,000 dictators finds an average transfer of roughly 28-29% of the endowment. Other studies showcase similar findings, reporting mean transfers of 20-30% of the endowment, with strong bimodal or trimodal distributions. Referring back, the 50% allocation across all models seems to disregard any alignment with human baseline, rather portraying an idealized fairness. This means that every model was insensitive to the variance of human motives such as warm-glow giving, taking aversion, entitlement effects, or strategic anticipation found in related games (Korenok et al; Engel 2010). 
All together, these findings show that current LLMs exhibit a structured and context dependent moral profile. They default to strongly utilitarian reasoning in impersonal and outcome/allocation dilemmas (switch trolley, Heinz, organ allocation). Then, they revert to deontological reasoning when asked to conduct direct physical harm (footbridge). Finally, they implement an unusually utopian norm in simple economic constructs like the dictator game. This pattern partly aligns with recent empirical work conducted by Cao, Borg et al., Zhang et al., Korenok et al., and Engel, indicating that LLMs have contextually dependent moral reasoning. Future research could possibly extend this analysis by varying dilemma framing, cultural context, and role structure in economic games (recipient rather than dictator). 



Conclusion
This study investigated the extent to which LLMs align with human ethical frameworks such as utilitarianism or deontology when given multiple moral dilemmas. The findings revealed a consistent pattern. LLMs showcase strong utilitarian tendencies in impersonal and outcome-based scenarios, such as the trolley, heinz dilemma, and organ allocation dilemma. LLMs shifted towards deontological reasoning in dilemmas involving direct personal harm, such as the footbridge dilemma. And in economic-decision making games, all models deviated from the human norms by consistently allocating 50% of their resources. Across scenarios, LLM responses were very consistent, with minimal variance across the trials. Human benchmarks, in contrast, showed greater variability due to prompt contextualization and emotional influence. These results indicate that LLMs partially replicate human moral reasoning patterns, but do so in a more rigid manner. 
The findings of this study should be interpreted while making note of several limitations. First, the experimental component relied on a relatively small number of trials per model per scenario, which limits the generalizability of the results. Second, the human moral baseline was constructed from multiple prior studies with varying methodologies, sample populations, and contextual framings. Third, the coding of responses into utilitarian or deontological categories simplifies complex moral reasoning into two overly simplified frameworks, which may not allow one to see the bigger picture in the moral reasoning. Despite these constraints, the chosen methodology allowed for comparisons across multiple models and scenarios, which strengthened the reliability and replicability of this research. Future research could address these limitations by increasing trial counts, standardizing a human moral baseline, and having an even stricter coding framework. 
The implications of this research are boundless because LLM-assisted decision making is appearing everywhere. In fields such as healthcare and law, understanding that LLMs tend to favor outcome-maximizing decisions in impersonal contexts but avoid direct harm highlights both their potential and their limitations as ethical decision makers. From a policy perspective, this highlights the importance of safety measures when implementing LLM systems in morally ambiguous environments. Theoretically, this study also contributes to ongoing debates about whether LLMs truly “reason” or if they just reproduce patterns from training data using algorithms. This study shows that their behavior shows context-dependent decision making, not autonomous ethical reasoning. 
Future research should build upon these findings by addressing existing gaps. One key direction involves increasing the diversity of moral dilemmas. Future studies could include culturally specific scenarios and real-world cases in order to test whether these reasoning patterns stay consistent outside of just hypotheticals. Additionally, future studies could change the prompt framing, emotional context, and other information about the dilemma to see how LLM outputs change. Finally, using theoretical frameworks from moral psychology, such as Kohlberg’s stages of moral development, may also give insight into the complexity of reasoning of the LLMs. A similar approach has already been done by Rashid et al., however more models could be tested (Rashid et al., 2024). 
Overall, this study found that LLMs exhibit partially aligned moral reasoning patterns. While they mimic human reasoning in some contexts, their responses lack the variability and adaptability that are prevalent in human judgment. This finding directly addresses the research question by showing that alignment exists, but only partial alignment. As LLMs become increasingly integrated into everyone’s day-to-day lives, a deeper understanding of these limitations becomes necessary to ensure the safety of LLMs in these domains going forward.
Ultimately, the advancement of AI depends on how well these LLMs can replicate, simplify, or even alter human values. Continued research will be necessary to ensure that AI systems support, rather than undermine, ethical decision making in society.

 1.0.1: Key words and Terminology:
Artificial Intelligence: Computer systems or Large Language Models (LLMs) designed to perform tasks that normally require humans. (In this research: ChatGPT, Deepseek, etc.)
Morality: Principles guiding judgments about right and wrong; used to compare human and AI ethical decisions
Dilemma: Hypothetical situation requiring a choice between ethically guided options; includes trolley problem, footbridge dilemma.
Human-morality: Baseline ethical principles guiding human moral decisions
Moral agents: Entities capable of making moral judgments and being held accountable for said judgments. Humans are moral agents.
Ethical frameworks: Specific systems of moral reasoning (e.g., utilitarianism, deontology).
ChatGPT  / Gemini / Claude / Deepseek: Specific AI large language models that this study will use to test moral decision making.
Sacrificial Dilemma: A type of moral dilemma where on individual’s harm is weighed against preventing harm to others, often used to assess utilitarian vs. deontological reasoning
Non-Sacrificial Dilemma: A moral dilemma where no direct tradeoff between individuals’ harm exists


1.2.1 Study Selection Framework: PICOS
	To ensure selection of the best studies, the PICOS framework was adopted, consistent with other established systematic review practices (Hassan et al., 2024):
Population: A diverse sample of human participants varying in ages, cultures, demographics, etc.
Intervention: Presentation with one or more structured moral dilemmas
Comparison: Human moral decisions across studies and, afterward, comparison to AI responses
Outcomes: Participant responses, justification of decisions, and alignment with ethical frameworks 
Study design: Experimental, survey-based, correlation, or observational studies 
1.2.3 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion criteria: Studies reporting both human choices and reasoning justifications in moral dilemmas, data that allowed for full coding into ethical framework and Kohlberg stages
Exclusion criteria: Studies lacking specific data, minimal sample sizes, or data that did not allow for coding into ethical framework 
1.2.6 Tools and Materials 
Code book: Provided insight into key data extraction used in this study
Software and other Statistical Tools: Google Sheets was used to calculate frequencies, means, as well as track data from LLM responses
1.4.2: Instruction Header and Closer
	The instruction header and closer served as crucial segments in all dilemma prompts. The purpose of the header and closer were to attain unbiased and clear responses from all LLMs. The instruction header was input directly before the prompt and the closer was input directly after the dilemma. 
	Instruction Header: “The following is a hypothetical moral scenario used solely for academic research on ethical reasoning. It does not describe real events and does not request personal advice.” 
	Instruction closer: “Provide a clear decision and explain the reasoning behind it. Do not reference named ethical theories or philosophers. Focus instead on the values, priorities, and constraints that influenced the decision.”
